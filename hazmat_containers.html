SHAPES — Container Shapes Quick Reference

Chart in words

  • Rounded ends: usually pressurized contents.
  • Flat ends: usually nonpressurized.
  • Spherical: high-pressure gases such as LPG at fixed sites.
  • Thermos-bottle shape: cryogenic liquids.
  • Cone bottom: dry bulk solids.
  • Rectangular: freight containers with mixed packaged cargo.

Shape is a clue only. Confirm with placards, UN numbers, and shipping papers.

FIXED FACILITY — Storage Tanks

Chart in words

  • Atmospheric (about 0 to 0.5 psi): flat-bottom tanks with cone or floating roofs; flammable and combustible liquids.
  • Low pressure (0.5 to 15 psi): horizontal tanks on saddles.
  • Pressure vessels (over 15 psi): rounded ends, relief devices; LPG and other gases.
  • Cryogenic: insulated, often vertical; refrigerated liquids.
  • Dry bulk: silos with cone bottoms.

RAIL CARS — Tank Cars
Chart removed: the earlier poster for this topic contained errors, so its key information is shown here as text.

Tank car types

  • Nonpressure (general service), such as the DOT-111: liquids and some solids; fittings visible on top.
  • Pressure, such as the DOT-112 and DOT-114: gases and liquefied gases; fittings inside a protective housing on top.
  • Cryogenic: refrigerated liquids; insulated tank within a tank.

Markings

  • Reporting marks and car number (for example GATX 123456) identify the car's owner. Marks ending in X belong to private (non-railroad) owners. No letter in the reporting mark tells you what is inside.
  • Specification marking (for example DOT-111A100W1) describes how the tank was built and tested. It does not identify the contents.
  • Contents: read the placard and UN number, the commodity name stenciled on dedicated cars, and the shipping papers (train consist).
Course answer discrepancy: earlier study material said a railcar number ending in "W" means hazardous waste. Reporting marks identify the owner, not the cargo; identify contents from placards, UN numbers, and shipping papers.
INTERMODAL — Containers
Chart removed: the earlier poster for this topic contained errors, so its key information is shown here as text.
  • Freight containers carry packaged goods; check placards on all four sides and the shipping papers.
  • Intermodal tank containers (portable tanks in a frame) carry liquids and gases in bulk; look for the UN number on the placard or orange panel.
  • The owner code on an intermodal container ends in U and identifies the owner, not the contents.
  • Portable tank codes such as "T11" are design instructions for the tank. They do not rank how hazardous the contents are.
PIPELINES — Markers & Response

Chart in words

  • Markers show the product, the operator, and an emergency phone number. They don't show the exact location or depth of the line.
  • Leak clues: dead vegetation, bubbling water, hissing, frost on the ground, odors, vapor clouds.
  • Isolate, keep people upwind, eliminate ignition sources, and call the pipeline operator. Don't operate valves unless the operator directs it.
NONBULK — Packaging
Chart removed: the earlier poster for this topic contained errors, so its key information is shown here as text.

Bulk vs. nonbulk (49 CFR 171.8)

Bulk packaging holds the material with no intermediate form of containment and has:

  • Liquids: capacity more than 450 L (119 gal)
  • Solids: net mass more than 400 kg (882 lb) or capacity more than 450 L (119 gal)
  • Gases: water capacity more than 454 kg (1,000 lb)

Anything at or below those limits is nonbulk: drums, cylinders, bags, carboys, boxes, Dewar flasks.

IBCs (totes) can hold up to 3,000 L (about 793 gal). A typical 275- or 330-gallon liquid tote is bulk packaging because it holds more than 119 gal.

Course answer discrepancy: earlier study material listed IBCs as nonbulk packaging of 119 gallons or less. Most liquid IBCs exceed 119 gallons and are bulk packaging.
TON CONTAINERS — & Y Cylinders
Chart removed: the earlier poster for this topic contained errors, so its key information is shown here as text.

Ton containers are horizontal pressure vessels holding about one ton of a liquefied gas such as chlorine. Valves are on one end under a protective hood; fusible plugs on the ends relieve pressure in a fire. Never tamper with them.

Chlorine (UN1017, ERG 2024 Guide 124, highlighted green)

SituationInitial isolation (all directions)
Small spill (Table 1)60 m (200 ft); then protect downwind 0.2 mi by day, 0.9 mi at night
Large spill, single ton cylinder or multiple small cylinders (Table 3)500 ft, plus downwind distances by wind speed
Large spill, multiple ton cylinders (Table 3)1,000 ft, plus downwind distances by wind speed

Isolation is a circle around the release. Protective action distances are a separate downwind area.

MARINE — Vessels

Chart in words

  • Tankers carry bulk liquids (crude oil, refined products, chemicals); gas carriers carry LNG or LPG; cargo ships carry containers and packaged goods.
  • The cargo manifest (dangerous cargo manifest) is the shipping paper for vessels.
  • Coordinate with the vessel operator and the U.S. Coast Guard; report spills to the National Response Center.
